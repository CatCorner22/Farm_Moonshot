DESCRIPTIONS OF CENSUS ENUMERATION DISTRICTS, 1830-1890 AND 1910-1950  
 
 Introduction 
 
This microfilm reproduces on 156 rolls of 35mm microfilm 513 original volumes of descriptions 
of geographic subdivisions (enumeration districts) that were used in the decennial United States 
censuses from 1830 to 1950.  These records are part of Record Group 29, Records of the Bureau 
of Census.  
 
The census subdivision or enumeration district served as the basic geographic area of a size that 
could be covered by a single census taker (enumerator) within one census period.  It may have 
been as large as one county or as small as part of a city block, depending on the density of 
population.  The term "subdivision," referring to a part of the supervisor's or marshal's division or 
district, was used in the early censuses.  With the 1880 census the term was changed to 
"enumeration district" and the census taker was officially called an "enumerator." 
 
For the censuses of 1830 and 1840 the two volumes included in this microfilm are entitled 
Accounts of Compensation to Assistant Marshals. They contain brief statements identifying the 
geographic jurisdictions covered by each census taker, as well as his name and rate of pay.  Later 
volumes from 1850 to 1900 show both "names of persons employed as enumerators" as well as 
"descriptions of their subdivisions."  Beginning in 1910 the names of the enumerators were no 
longer included in the volumes, which by then were called Descriptions of Enumeration Districts. 
 The records are further described in entries 2, 3, 13, 27, 42 and 189 of National Archives 
Preliminary Inventory No 161, Records of the Bureau of Census. 
 
A table of contents for each census year has been provided.  The original volumes are arranged 
by assigned volume number, beginning anew with each census year.  Within individual volumes 
the pages are typically arranged numerically by supervisor's or marshal's district and thereunder 
numerically by subdivision or enumeration district.  This arrangement often conforms to an 
alphabetical arrangement by county.  Major cities are sometimes arranged separately from the 
counties.  As an aid to the user the 1850 and 1860 volumes, which have little order in the 
original, have been filmed out of the original page sequence so that the states and territories 
appear on film in alphabetical order. 
 
The amount of detail shown in the volumes varies greatly from year to year, from a brief

statement of the town or jurisdiction covered by the census taker to the streets and landmarks that 
form the boundaries.  In the earlier years the distinction between two subdivisions is not always 
clear, as in the examples "Part of Bristol County" and "Remainder of Bristol County."  Typically 
included are the number of the subdivision (enumeration district), the county, township, city, 
ward, borough or other civil division that comprises it or a description of its boundaries, the 
number of square miles it contains and the name of the assistant marshal, census taker or 
enumerator with his post office address and rate of pay (1830 to 1900 only).  The number of 
returns from each subdivision (population, dwellings or farms from the ongoing or previous 
census) is sometimes included.  The 1870 volume includes an "industrial description" that 
indicates whether the economy of the subdivision is primarily commercial, residential, industrial 
or mining. 
 
The 1900, 1940 and 1950 volumes were microfilmed prior to the preparation of this final 
microfilm.  The 1900 volumes were the first to be filmed by the National Archives and were 
originally assigned roll numbers 1-10, which now have been renumbered to follow the 
chronological sequence of the remaining film (see table of contents).  The 1940 and 1950 
volumes were also given obsolete roll numbers when they were filmed while still in the Bureau 
of the Census before they were deposited in the National Archives and they also have been 
assigned new roll numbers. 
 
After 1880 it is often helpful to use the descriptions in conjunction with the enumeration district 
maps that exist in the National Archives.  The maps cover only a few counties for the 1880 and 
1890 censuses, but cover nearly all of the counties and cities for the later censuses of 1900 to 
1960.  The maps were marked by hand in the census offices at the time of each census to show 
the boundaries that each enumerator was to follow.  These maps are reproducible on request, 
although their size often precludes their being microfilmed on 35mm film and their colored 
boundaries are difficult to interpret when reproduced as black and white copies.
